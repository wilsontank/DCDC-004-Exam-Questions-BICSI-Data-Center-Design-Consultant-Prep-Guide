<h2><strong>What Is the BICSI Data Center Design Consultant (DCDC) Certification?</strong></h2>

<p>The BICSI Data Center Design Consultant (DCDC) certification validates the ability to understand and coordinate the many systems, stakeholders, documents, standards, and operational requirements involved in data center projects. BICSI&rsquo;s data center design programs focus on planning, implementing, operating, and making critical data center decisions, including site selection, space planning, electrical systems, mechanical systems, telecommunications, security, fire protection, building automation, commissioning, and maintenance.</p>

<p>Modern data centers are highly complex facilities. A data center consultant must understand not only ICT systems, but also architectural space design, utilities, power, cooling, physical security, operations, disaster recovery, construction coordination, and commissioning. The DCDC certification helps demonstrate that a professional can support data center projects from early concept planning through design, construction, handover, and ongoing operational review.</p>

<p>The DCDC-004 exam is suitable for experienced ICT and data center professionals who participate in data center design, construction, operations, consulting, or project coordination.</p>

<h3><span style="font-size:16px"><strong>Concept Planning and Analysis</strong></span></h3>

<p><strong>Exam Weight: 30%</strong></p>

<p>This is the most heavily weighted area of the DCDC-004 exam. It focuses on the early planning stage of a data center project. Candidates must understand how to conduct a needs assessment, identify client objectives, determine operational requirements, evaluate data center models, and assess whether a cloud, colocation, on-premises, or other model is appropriate.</p>

<p>Key topics include:</p>

<ul>
	<li>Conducting client needs assessments</li>
	<li>Determining operational and availability requirements</li>
	<li>Evaluating cloud, colocation, and on-premises models</li>
	<li>Identifying future growth and project phasing</li>
	<li>Reviewing shell space and expansion planning</li>
	<li>Conducting site surveys and site research</li>
	<li>Evaluating natural environment risks</li>
	<li>Reviewing utility environment factors such as power, water, gas, telecommunications, and cost</li>
	<li>Understanding financial implications and tax incentives</li>
	<li>Developing feasibility reports</li>
</ul>

<p>This domain is important because poor planning can create long-term problems in capacity, resiliency, cost, sustainability, and operational performance.</p>

<h3><span style="font-size:16px"><strong>Architectural, Mechanical, and Electrical Systems</strong></span></h3>

<p><strong>Exam Weight: 20%</strong></p>

<p>This area focuses on the physical facility systems that support the data center. Candidates should understand how architectural space design, mechanical systems, and electrical systems affect the overall data center design.</p>

<p>The architectural and space design portion requires candidates to apply client requirements and feasibility analysis to create functional layouts. This includes planning IT equipment space, support areas, personnel requirements, security areas, and adjacencies.</p>

<p>Mechanical systems include utility considerations such as water use, fuel storage, gas, sewer, and stormwater. Electrical systems include medium-voltage service, underground or overhead utility delivery, backup power, distribution systems, and voltage criteria. A DCDC candidate must know how to coordinate these requirements with engineers to ensure the design supports the client&rsquo;s intent.</p>

<h3><span style="font-size:16px"><strong>IT, Ancillary Systems, and Communications Connectivity</strong></span></h3>

<p><strong>Exam Weight: 20%</strong></p>

<p>This domain covers the technology and support systems inside the data center. Candidates should understand how IT equipment, storage, servers, networks, fire protection, physical security, building automation systems, and communications connectivity affect the detailed design.</p>

<p>Key areas include:</p>

<ul>
	<li>Network, storage, and server requirements</li>
	<li>Cabinets, racks, SAN, standalone equipment, and network security</li>
	<li>Fire protection systems</li>
	<li>Physical security systems</li>
	<li>Building automation systems</li>
	<li>Fiber, copper, pathways, and communications requirements</li>
	<li>Coordination with RCDD professionals and other discipline designers</li>
</ul>

<p>This section requires cross-discipline thinking. A data center design consultant must coordinate IT requirements with architectural, electrical, mechanical, security, and communications teams to ensure the facility functions as a complete system.</p>

<h3><span style="font-size:16px"><strong>Data Center Operations and Maintenance Assessment</strong></span></h3>

<p><strong>Exam Weight: 10%</strong></p>

<p>This section focuses on assessing the current state and ongoing performance of a data center. Candidates must understand how to evaluate operational efficiency, capacity, growth potential, performance, utilization, utility costs, physical security, maintenance operations, documentation, and safety procedures.</p>

<p>Important topics include:</p>

<ul>
	<li>Conducting optimization studies</li>
	<li>Assessing mechanical, electrical, and ancillary system conditions</li>
	<li>Reviewing efficiency and capacity</li>
	<li>Evaluating aging technologies and refresh needs</li>
	<li>Assessing utilities and operational costs</li>
	<li>Reviewing physical security</li>
	<li>Evaluating maintenance operations and procedures</li>
	<li>Reviewing documentation such as commissioning reports, logs, and DCIM records</li>
	<li>Assessing safety procedures such as lockout/tagout</li>
	<li>Developing assessment reports</li>
</ul>

<p>This domain is practical because many DCDC professionals work not only on new data centers, but also on upgrades, audits, optimization projects, and operational assessments.</p>

<h3><span style="font-size:16px"><strong>Security Assessment</strong></span></h3>

<p><strong>Exam Weight: 10%</strong></p>

<p>Security is a critical part of data center design. This domain validates the ability to support threat assessments, security audits, countermeasure planning, and disaster recovery design recommendations.</p>

<p>Candidates should understand how to:</p>

<ul>
	<li>Manage threat assessments</li>
	<li>Identify threat frequency and impact</li>
	<li>Participate in security audits and building inspections</li>
	<li>Verify audit findings against security objectives</li>
	<li>Identify physical, electronic, and organizational countermeasures</li>
	<li>Coordinate cost-benefit or feasibility analysis</li>
	<li>Translate disaster recovery requirements into recovery design recommendations</li>
</ul>

<p>This area is important because data centers house mission-critical systems. Physical security, electronic security, operational controls, and recovery planning must be considered as part of the full design process.</p>

<h3><span style="font-size:16px"><strong>Construction Administration and Commissioning</strong></span></h3>

<p><strong>Exam Weight: 10%</strong></p>

<p>The final exam area focuses on the project execution and validation phases. Candidates should understand how a data center consultant supports the bid process, construction coordination, project closeout, pre-commissioning, commissioning planning, and commissioning execution.</p>

<p>Construction administration topics include:</p>

<ul>
	<li>Assisting with bid process and award</li>
	<li>Coordinating with clients and contractors during construction</li>
	<li>Supporting project closeout activities</li>
</ul>

<p>Commissioning topics include:</p>

<ul>
	<li>Supporting pre-commissioning activities</li>
	<li>Verifying commissioning tests against design requirements</li>
	<li>Assisting with commissioning plan development</li>
	<li>Reviewing commissioning results against design intent</li>
</ul>

<p>BICSI&#39;s data center learning materials emphasize that data center projects involve the complete lifecycle from conceptualization to planning, implementation, and project closeout.</p>
